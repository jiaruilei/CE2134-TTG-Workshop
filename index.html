SPEAKING SCRIPT
My starting point is experiential learning. Physical experience and digital exploration give students something to observe and question. The Bernoulli Pipe platform brings the simulation and AI Coach together. Students first predict an answer, explore the simulator and compare the result with their expectation. They then ask a question, check the response and explain any revision. A check can use a governing equation, units, a limiting case or a graph. The sequence finishes with a new problem for independent work. Prediction, verification and explanation are the core of this learning activity.

SPEAKING SCRIPT
The API-tutor group produced stronger written response checks in PSet 1. Students were asked to test a chatbot reply using a relevant physical argument, such as an equation, units, a limiting case or the graph. The API-tutor group averaged 1.74 out of two, compared with 1.45 for the rule-based group. The difference was 0.28 marks, with a 95 percent interval from 0.06 to 0.50. This makes response verification a clear focus for the teaching design. The total submitted-work scores averaged 22.12 and 21.87 out of 24, a difference of 0.25 marks. Final numerical correctness was approximately 98 percent in both groups. The response-checking task therefore gave us more information about how students evaluated feedback than the final numerical answers alone. In the next evaluation, verification should be a clearly defined outcome with independent marking.

Confidence increased during PSet 2. Among the 47 students with paired ratings, the mean rose from 4.06 to 4.37 on a five-point scale. SPEAKING SCRIPT
The PSet 3 quiz comparison is sensitive to how activity is weighted. When all attempts are pooled, accuracy is 81.7 percent for browser IDs with any recorded AI reply and 61.3 percent for IDs with no matched reply. This gives a difference of 20.4 percentage points. When every ID receives equal weight, the difference becomes minus 11.7 points. A further sensitivity analysis excludes three rapid-answer IDs and gives a difference of minus 0.5 points. All three confidence intervals include zero. The result changes because highly active IDs contribute more to the pooled calculation. These logs are useful for describing practice behaviour and identifying patterns that deserve closer review. For the next evaluation, student-linked first attempts and a common assessment would provide a more consistent basis for comparing performance.

SPEAKING SCRIPT
The dialogue records show how AI supported clarification and answer correction. In this pipe-flow example, the initial downstream velocity was 1.2 metres per second. The feedback explained continuity and Bernoulli and supplied a velocity of 2.7 metres per second. About nine minutes later, the logged velocity and pressure had been revised to the values shown here. A second trace showed a correct Pitot response after clarification of the appropriate method. These are concrete examples of support at a point of uncertainty. The records also identify a numerical-consistency problem: one reply rejected a pressure of 116.6 kilopascals and then calculated 116.625, which agrees at the stated precision. That example makes the verification task very tangible. Students should check both the physical explanation and the numerical judgment.

SPEAKING SCRIPT
These findings point to a specific teaching sequence. Students should commit to an answer, identify the governing physics, check the feedback and explain their revision. Verification becomes a visible part of the work they submit. The next evaluation should connect these steps through pseudonymous student records, so that first answers and later corrections can be examined separately. A common unaided task with blinded marking would assess independent reasoning, and a delayed test would assess retention. Confidence should be recorded for the same item to allow comparison with correctness. The proposed comparison is rule-based support, conversational AI and verification-first AI, using the same content and practice time. This design would focus the evaluation on the contribution of structured checking. The teaching priority is clear: preserve the interactive experience and make students’ reasoning explicit at each stage.

The main finding is that the API-tutor group produced stronger written checks of chatbot responses. Confidence also increased during PSet 2 among students with paired ratings, and the dialogue records show concrete examples of clarification and answer correction. These findings give us a practical direction for AI-assisted learning: make students predict, check and explain. A new problem completed independently is the next assessment step, followed by a delayed test of retention. For me, the value of the platform lies in the opportunity to examine how students reason and how they respond to feedback.
